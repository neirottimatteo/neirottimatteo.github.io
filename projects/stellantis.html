04 — Work · 2023–2025

Crash-test data

Stellantis Safety Center, Orbassano as a consultant with ALTEN Italia January 2023 — September 2025

A crash test is a one-shot event. A real vehicle, instrumented dummies, high-speed cameras, and an impact that lasts less than a tenth of a second and destroys everything it touches. You can only repeat it by buying another car. The only thing that survives the impact is the data — and for nearly three years that data was my responsibility.

The context

The Safety Center in Orbassano is where Stellantis verifies the passive safety of its vehicles: homologation tests, independent rating protocols, and the long tail of internal development testing that precedes every model. I arrived there at twenty, as an ALTEN consultant, and stayed until September 2025 as Test Data Management & Integration Lead.

Every test generates a volume of measurement that surprises people outside the field: dozens upon dozens of channels from accelerometers, load cells and strain gauges on the dummies and on the vehicle structure, sampled at very high rates, plus high-speed video synchronised to the instant of impact. All of it has to be acquired correctly on the first attempt, labelled unambiguously, retained for years, and remain readable by people and tools that were not in the room when the test happened.

The mandate: data that can be trusted

The Center operates under ISO 17025 accreditation — the international standard defining the competence requirements for testing laboratories. In practice that means it is not enough for a measurement to be right: it has to be demonstrably right. Every measurement chain must be traceable, every instrument calibrated, every method validated, every record reconstructable years later in front of an external auditor.

It is a context that changes how you think about software. In a normal product, a data processing bug is an annoyance. Here, a mislabelled channel, a wrong scale factor or a timing misalignment can propagate into a safety compliance report. The cost of an error is not measured in tickets.

My job, at its base, was to guarantee three things simultaneously: that the data was accurate, that it was accessible to whoever needed it in the format they needed, and that the whole chain would survive an audit. Those three are in more tension than they sound.

The main project: moving everything onto ISO 13499

The work I am proudest of was owning the standardisation and migration of data-acquisition systems and formats onto the ISO 13499 standard, the international norm for exchanging crash-test data — channel measurements and multimedia, together with the metadata describing the test, the vehicle, the occupants and the instrumentation.

Why it matters: a laboratory that stores its data in a proprietary format builds itself a prison. Data cannot be exchanged easily with suppliers, partners or external bodies; every new analysis tool needs a bespoke converter; and an archive of destroyed tests becomes unreadable if the software that wrote it no longer exists. An open standard solves all three at once.

The project was half technical and half organisational. On the technical side: mapping what the existing systems actually produced, identifying where they diverged from the standard, defining the conversion rules, and verifying that nothing was lost in translation. On the organisational side: getting different systems, vendors and people to adopt the same convention — which is the hard part, because each of them already had a way of working that suited them.

I also kept continuous watch on how the format evolved: standards get revised, test protocols change, and a laboratory that stops tracking them drifts out of compliance without noticing.

Being the translator between three worlds

In the middle of a test laboratory there are three worlds that do not naturally speak the same language. The sensor database, which knows every transducer, its calibration and its history. The acquisition hardware, which writes the raw data during the impact. And the analysis tools, which read it back to produce curves, injury criteria and reports.

Each of those worlds is owned by different people, with different constraints and different vocabularies. My role was to coordinate with the development teams so that interoperability was real and not merely claimed: that the channel identified one way in the database was the same channel with the same name in the acquired file and in the analysis tool, that sign conventions and units matched, and that every system and application genuinely adhered to ISO 13499.

A good part of that work was writing explicit acceptance criteria and then checking them. An integration nobody verifies is an assumption, not an integration.

Python automation

When I arrived, a significant share of the data work was manual: repetitive steps, done by hand, where error is not a possibility but a statistical certainty. I designed and built a set of Python applications to remove them.

  • Ingestion and conversion — bringing acquisition system output into the standard format, repeatably and with a documented trail.
  • Automated validation — systematic checks on structure, metadata, channel naming and consistency, run before the data leaves the lab rather than after.
  • Compatibility with new tools — preparing and verifying data whenever a new analysis tool came into use, so that adopting it did not require re-working the archive by hand.
  • Reporting support — analysing test data and producing the reports for safety compliance, with the mechanical part automated and human attention reserved for technical judgement.

The goal was not speed for its own sake. It was that every step became verifiable: an automated process fails the same way every time, which means you can find it, fix it and prove that you fixed it. A manual process fails differently every time.

Leading the team

Over time the role became less individual and more coordinating: leading a team on test data management, defining how the work gets done, making sure the standard was respected by everyone rather than just by me, and acting as the point of contact towards the development teams and towards the test engineers.

On top of that sat day-to-day technical support — resolving the hardware and software problems that, in a laboratory, never wait for a convenient moment. It is not work that goes on a slide, but it is what keeps the machine running on a day when a test is scheduled.

I walked in at twenty, as an external consultant, into an accredited laboratory with decades of established practice. I walked out nearly three years later having led the migration of its data formats onto an international standard. The lesson only sounds trivial when written down: technical credibility is not requested, it is built by solving the problems other people were avoiding.

What I took with me

Everything I built afterwards carries the imprint of that laboratory. CRONO does not simply claim an accuracy: it measures itself against a professional reference and publishes the number. The Recession Probability Engine does not ask you to trust a probability: it decomposes every prediction indicator by indicator. Fira does not summarise the news: it goes back to the original document filed with the SEC.

It is always the same idea, learned in a room where cars are driven into a wall: a number is worth exactly as much as the chain that produced it.
