04 — 2023–2025

Stellantis Safety Center

Test Data Management & Integration Lead Orbassano · as a consultant with ALTEN Italia January 2023 — September 2025

A crash test cannot be repeated. A real vehicle, instrumented dummies, high-speed cameras, and an impact lasting less than a tenth of a second. Running it again means buying another car. What remains is the data, and for almost three years the data was my job.

The context

The Safety Center in Orbassano is where Stellantis verifies the passive safety of its vehicles: homologation tests, independent rating protocols, and the long tail of development testing that precedes every model. I arrived there at twenty as an ALTEN consultant and stayed until September 2025.

Every test produces far more data than you would guess from outside: dozens of channels across accelerometers, load cells and strain gauges on the dummies and on the structure, sampled at very high rates, plus high-speed video synchronised to the moment of impact. All of it has to be acquired correctly on the first attempt, labelled without ambiguity, kept for years, and stay readable by people and tools that were not in the test hall that day.

The mandate

The Center operates under ISO 17025 accreditation, the standard defining the competence requirements for testing laboratories. In practice a measurement does not only have to be right: it has to be demonstrably right, years later, in front of an external auditor. Traceable measurement chains, calibrated instruments, validated methods, reconstructable records.

My job was to hold together three things that pull against each other: accurate data, data accessible to whoever needed it in the format they needed, and a chain that would survive an audit.

The main project: moving everything onto ISO 13499

When I arrived, the test data was not standardised. Each acquisition system wrote in its own way, channel names followed internal conventions layered up over the years, and every analysis tool needed its own adapter. It worked, in the sense that the tests got done — but every exchange with the outside world and every new tool cost manual work.

ISO 13499 is the international standard for exchanging crash-test data. It defines how a test is structured: measurement channels in separate files, the metadata describing test, vehicle, occupants, instrumentation and conditions, the multimedia material, and an index that holds it all together. The benefit is not cosmetic. A laboratory writing in a proprietary format builds itself a prison: data cannot be exchanged with suppliers, partners and external bodies without conversion; every new tool needs a bespoke converter; and an archive of destroyed tests becomes unreadable the day the software that wrote it no longer exists.

The technical work was mapping what the systems in use actually produced, finding where they diverged from the standard, defining the conversion rules channel by channel, and verifying that nothing was lost in the move: units, scale factors, sign conventions, timing relative to the moment of impact, calibration information, filters applied. These are the details nobody sees until someone tries to reopen a test from two years earlier.

I also kept watch on how the standard and the test protocols evolved over time. A laboratory that stops following them drifts out of compliance without noticing.

I was the owner of the project

The technical part was half the job. The other half was holding the people together. The project cut across different groups with different roles: the people who own the sensor database, and know every transducer, its calibration and its history; the people who look after the acquisition hardware, which writes the raw data during the impact; the people who develop and maintain the analysis tools, which read it back to produce curves, injury criteria and reports. And above all the people in the test hall: the ones who place the sensors, prepare the vehicle and the dummies, and run the test.

As owner of the project the important thing was keeping a wide view over all of it, so that nothing got lost: no forgotten conversion, no edge case left behind, no problem raised by one group that never reached another. Each group saw one piece, and most of the problems sat exactly at the joints between one piece and the next.

The needs of the people actually running the tests mattered as much as the technical ones, sometimes more. A system that respects the standard but slows down the work in the test hall does not get adopted — it gets worked around. So a good share of the project was understanding how they really worked, what slowed them down, where labelling errors came from, and building something that made their day easier rather than adding steps. That was not simple, and it took a lot of back and forth.

A concrete part of the work was writing explicit acceptance criteria and then going and checking them, test after test, system after system. An integration nobody verifies stays an assumption.

The Python tooling

Many steps were manual and repetitive, and repeated manual steps produce errors with statistical regularity. I designed and built a set of Python applications to take them out of the loop.

  • Conversion — bringing acquisition system output into the standard format, repeatably and with a documented trail.
  • Automated validation — systematic checks on structure, metadata, channel naming and consistency, run before the data leaves the lab rather than after.
  • Compatibility with new tools — preparing and verifying data whenever a new analysis tool came into use, so that adopting it did not mean reworking the archive by hand.

The point was not speed. An automated process fails the same way every time, so you can find it, fix it, and prove you fixed it. A manual process fails differently every time.

I read and analysed the compliance reports myself: that part needed technical judgement and was never automated. I also did some technical support when something broke, though that was not my main job.

Where it ended up

When I arrived the system was not standardised. When I left, a good share of the tests were being run in ISO 13499 and the tools whose construction I had managed were in daily use.

It is the work I am proudest of. Not for the technical part, which was interesting but manageable, but because changing how an accredited laboratory works, with years of settled practice behind it, requires the people who work there to see something in it for themselves. That was the hard part, and that is the part that worked.
