Recession Probability Engine

Kleriox research Python · public U.S. data Paper on GitHub ↗

Version 5.0, November 2026. The reading is updated every month and the public paper is on GitHub.

Every month this model gives one number: the probability that the United States will be in recession within the next twelve months. It uses public economic data. I tested it on the past, one month at a time, using only what was known at that moment. On that test it caught all six recessions between 1971 and 2025, with 11.8 months of warning on average.

What it does

The model publishes one probability a month, for the last complete month, and only when everything that reading depends on has been released. The reading is smoothed, so a single noisy month does not push it over the line. Each reading comes with a short written explanation of what moved it.

It does not say how deep or how long a recession will be, and it is not a trading system. It is an early-warning signal.

Why it is hard

Four things make it harder than it looks.

  • Few examples. There have been only about a dozen US recessions since the Second World War. In most machine-learning work that would be a tiny sample.
  • Late dating. The NBER, which dates US recessions, announces a start six to twelve months after it happened. For the most recent months the answer is not known yet.
  • A false alarm costs credibility. A model that warns all the time catches every recession and is useless, and after a false alarm the next real warning risks being ignored. I ordered the goals that way: no false alarms first, then as much warning as that allows.
  • Luck counts for a lot. With six recessions in the test, a result can look like skill and be chance. Most of the work in version 5 was telling a real improvement from a lucky one.

How I test it

A model that has seen the past can describe it almost perfectly, and that says nothing about real time. So every past month is predicted the way it would have been predicted then:

  • by a model trained only on earlier history;
  • using each figure only from the date it was actually released, since economic statistics come out weeks or months after the period they describe;
  • never using an outcome that would not yet have been known;
  • and scoring the reading as it is published, not a more flattering number underneath it.

Every change follows three rules. I write down what counts as an improvement before running the test, and the first condition is always that it adds no false alarm. I choose a change on the older years and check it once on the more recent years, which I do not look at until then. And a change that fails is recorded as not proven and is not adopted.

V4 is version 4 as it was published. The last column is the standard simple model from the literature (Wright, 2006), based on the yield curve and the level of short-term rates. I ran it through the identical test.

The benchmark warns earlier before 2008, and it pays for that with 28 false-alarm months, 21 of them in a row from April 2023 to December 2024, when the yield curve inverted and no recession followed. The yield curve is not wrong. On its own it has no context, and an inversion in a healthy economy is a different event from one that comes with a broad slowdown.

Recession by recession: each of the six is preceded by a sustained reading above 50%. The warning is there for the full 18 months I count before 1980 and 1981, for 15 months before 1990, 8 before 2001, 7 before 2008 and 5 before 1973.

The hardest test was 2022–23. The Federal Reserve raised rates by 525 basis points in 16 months, the yield curve inverted deeper than at any time since 1981, and many people expected a recession. The reading rose to 44.0% in September 2023 and never crossed 50%. No recession followed. It was not a comfortable margin, and I do not present it as one.

Version by version

I have published five versions, each with its own paper in the GitHub folder. The numbers before version 4 were measured differently, as averages over test periods, so I compare versions only from version 3 onward, on the published series.

V1
A first model with a rule that needed three months in a row above the line. It missed the 2001 recession.
V2
The reading is smoothed instead of waiting for three months in a row, which caught 2001. The data are also aligned to the dates they actually came out, which removed a quiet way of cheating with time. That fix moved the score very little, and I made it anyway.
V2.1
A stricter test: twelve months between training and testing, because the labels of those months already contain information about the test window. Results moved by less than half a point.
V3
The published model is built exactly like the one that was tested. Before, the live model was calmer than the ones used to set the 50% line, so the line was calibrated on one model and applied to another.
V4
Fixed a long false alarm in 1984–86 (21 months above 50%, peaking at 82%): false-alarm months went from 46 to 10, and every recession was still caught. Results are measured on the series exactly as it is published, and I added the yield-curve benchmark.
V5
The published model is the average of several training runs instead of one. False-alarm months 10 to 8, ROC-AUC 0.948 to 0.957, the 2001 warning 8 months ahead instead of 3. The 1990 and 2008 warnings come one month later. A second, separate set of runs reproduces the same leads and the same false-alarm months.

What was hard

The 1984–86 false alarm

Version 3 had a problem I could see in the chart: it warned for 21 months in the mid-1980s and nothing followed. The model covering those years had been trained only up to the 1981–82 recession. In its data, high interest rates always came before a recession, so it learned that rule with no exceptions. It had never seen high rates in a healthy economy. The fix changed how the models are trained. It had one free setting, which could have been adjusted until the problem disappeared, so I wrote down the rule for choosing it before running the test.

The luck of the draw

Training a model like this involves some randomness: train it twice on the same data and you get slightly different models. Through version 4 I published a single training run. When I repeated the whole test many times, I found that the claim “no false alarm since 1985” was true of the published run and would not have been true of more than a quarter of its siblings. I had published a lucky draw. From version 5 the published model is the average of several runs, fixed before their results were known.

2001

The 2001 recession is the weakest episode in the record. The reading first crosses 50% in August 2000, at 51.7%, dips below in October and is back above from November. A crossing that clears the line by under two points is a thin warning, and I say so in the paper. Single training runs give leads between four and eight months for it. What does not change is that the recession is caught on every run.

Ideas that worked, and ideas that did not

What worked:

  • Smoothing the reading. By far the biggest single improvement, and it caught 2001.
  • Changing how the test models are trained. The 1985 case: from 46 false-alarm months to 10.
  • Averaging several training runs. A calmer record, and no dependence on one draw.

What did not:

  • Adding more indicators. I tested many extra ones, including a stricter test in November. None did better than chance.
  • Longer yield-curve measures. They were among the best-scoring ideas on the older years, and looked promising. Checked once on the recent years, they would have kept the model at or above 50% for long stretches between 2023 and 2026, with no recession.
  • Removing indicators that a statistical test flagged as harmful. I removed some, the results got worse, and I put them back.

Everything that failed is recorded as not proven, and the model stays as it was.

How it was made

The idea and the questions are mine: what to predict, how to test it honestly, what counts as an improvement, which ideas to try next. I decided the rules and checked that the results made sense.

What it cannot do

  • Six recessions are a short record. Every number above has wide uncertainty.
  • A reading is a risk score against the 50% line, not a frequency. A reading of 30% does not mean a recession follows three times in ten.
  • Economic data are revised after their first release, and the test cannot fully reproduce what a reader saw in real time.
  • It says nothing about depth, length, sectors or markets.
  • An economy unlike anything in the record would be one the model has not seen.

The public paper, with all the results and tables, is on GitHub. It does not describe the method, which is proprietary.
